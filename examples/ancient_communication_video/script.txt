Here's a strange question: if you traveled back a hundred thousand years and said hello... nobody would understand a single word. Not because they were stupid — but because words had not been invented yet.

But how did we start talking at all? It began not with words, but with hands, faces, and sounds — pointing at danger, gasping at a lion, grunting for food.

And it worked — for a while. You could warn your tribe about a hunt. You could not, however, explain yesterday. Or a dream. Or why the sky turns red.

Speaking itself is ancient — at least ten times older than any writing. For most of human history, every story, every rule, every secret lived in one place only: somebody's memory.

Then humans found a cheat code: painting. Around forty thousand years ago, people began covering cave walls with animals and hunters — not for decoration, but to send messages that outlived the messenger.

Painting could freeze a moment in time. But try painting 'tomorrow we trade grain for goats' — pictures get blurry fast when ideas get complicated.

The next breakthrough came from accounting — seriously. About ten thousand years ago in Mesopotamia, farmers kept little clay tokens for sheep and grain. One token, one sheep. Counting became memory.

Then someone pressed the tokens into soft clay to record trades — and the marks of the tokens stayed behind. The first written symbols were literally accounting receipts.

Around five thousand years ago, those marks grew into real writing systems — cuneiform in the East, hieroglyphs in Egypt. Suddenly the river could carry a letter, and the desert could keep a law.

Here is the trick that made writing unstoppable: pictures stopped showing things and started showing sounds. A drawing of an eye could mean the sound 'eye' — and writing could finally capture names, jokes, and lies.

But writing was still a job for experts — hundreds of symbols to memorize. Then merchants invented something clever: about twenty-two letters. If you can learn the alphabet, you can write anything.

Everything changed at once. Kings wrote laws that applied to everyone. Merchants sent contracts across seas. Generals wrote histories — and the past stopped disappearing every generation.

Paper made writing cheap enough for letters, poems, and shopping lists. The printing press made it unstoppable — one press, thousands of identical pages, ideas that could outrun any ruler.

Then the telegraph pulled words across continents in seconds. The radio pulled them into every kitchen. The internet pulled them across everything — instantly, all at once, all the time.

Next time a message lands on your phone — a meme, a voice note, a simple 'where are you?' — remember what it really is: the great-grandchild of a handprint on a cave wall.

Communication is our oldest technology, and our most human one. Everything else — every law, every song, every science — began the day somebody first tried to say 'I am here' to somebody else.
