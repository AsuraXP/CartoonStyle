Why Ancient People Got Drunk

[SFX dusty scroll unrolling]

NARRATOR: If you traveled back two thousand years and asked for a glass of plain water, the person serving you would stare at you like you just asked to drink poison.

NARRATOR: Because for most of human history, water was a gamble. Rivers carried whatever the city upstream tossed into them, and nobody had ever heard of a filter.

NARRATOR: So what did everyone drink instead? Beer. Morning, noon, and night. Grown-ups, old people, even the kids got a mug with breakfast.

NARRATOR: And nobody was at a party. The beer was weak and slightly chunky, and it was basically liquid bread — food you could drink through a straw.

NARRATOR: In ancient Egypt, the crews who hauled the stones for the pyramids were not paid in gold. Their wages arrived in clay jugs of beer. Three jugs a day. It was written right into the accounting scrolls.

NARRATOR: And the wildest part? Nobody invented beer on purpose. Someone left grain and water sitting in a warm jar a little too long, and wild yeast floating in the air moved in uninvited.

NARRATOR: The yeast ate the sugar in the grain, and out came tiny bubbles and a little bit of alcohol. The drink stopped spoiling. The calories lasted for months. Fermentation was the first refrigerator.

[SFX bubbling pot]

NARRATOR: Bonus secret: brewing meant boiling the water. That killed the invisible germs that made everyone sick — centuries before anybody knew germs existed.

NARRATOR: The ancient Greeks and Romans went further and made wine. But even they almost never drank it pure at a banquet. They mixed it into a big bowl, usually three parts water to one part wine.

NARRATOR: Showing up drunk to a Roman dinner party was deeply embarrassing. Philosophers literally wrote essays scolding people who could not handle their cups.

NARRATOR: Up north, where grapes refused to grow in the snow, the Vikings brewed dark ale for the long winters, and a golden honey wine called mead for the celebrations.

NARRATOR: Sailors packed whole barrels of ale onto their ships, because after three weeks at sea the water barrel turned green and slimy. Nobody wanted to look inside it.

NARRATOR: In the Middle Ages, the average person drank about a liter of ale every single day. Breakfast came with a mug. It was just how the morning worked.

NARRATOR: And the best breweries in Europe were not taverns. They were monasteries, where monks treated brewing like sacred chemistry — part cooking, part prayer, part business.

NARRATOR: Then, one day, tea, coffee, and chocolate sailed in from faraway lands. Suddenly there was something warm and exciting to drink that did not get anyone tipsy.

NARRATOR: And just like that, entire cities slowly sobered up. The water stayed dangerous, but the mugs of beer quietly moved aside.

NARRATOR: So the next time somebody says the ancients loved to party, remember the truth. Most of the time, they were just trying very hard not to get sick from the water.

NARRATOR: The real drink of the ancient world was not fancy wine. It was a weak, warm, slightly chunky beer. Cheers to that.
