# Day 1 of the Zombie Apocalypse (I Was Working Register)

It was 6:47 PM. I was working register at the Stop-n-Go on Route 9. I was seven minutes from clocking out. I had already taken my apron off. The hot dog roller had been cold for two hours. I was restocking the cigarette rack and thinking about the frozen burrito in my microwave at home. That is the single most ordinary thought a human being can have. And it was the last ordinary thought I had for a very long time.

A man ran into the parking lot. Not jogging, not stumbling — sprinting, flat out, like he was being chased by something that could actually catch him. His shirt was torn. He was bleeding from the neck. He didn't look at me. He ran straight into the glass door face-first so hard I heard it crack. He bounced off, grabbed the handle with both hands, and started yanking it so hard I thought he'd pull the door off its frame. I stared at him through the glass for maybe two seconds. Then I looked past him.

There were more behind him. Dozens. Not the slow groaning kind you see in movies, arms out, shuffling. These were moving. Full sprint. Heads down. Arms pumping. Mouths open. They ran like they were furious. Like a crowd rushing a stadium gate when the team scores. They ran over a parked motorcycle without slowing down. They ran into each other and kept going. Some of them were covered in blood. Some of them were missing pieces. One of them was missing the entire lower half of its jaw and it was still running.

I locked the door. I don't remember deciding to. My hand just did it. I twisted the deadbolt. I flipped the security bar down. I grabbed the stack of wet floor signs and propped them against the glass because that is the stupidest thing any human has ever done in an emergency and it made sense to me at the time.

The first wave hit the door thirty seconds later. The entire building shook. A display of chip racks fell over. The man who had run into the glass was no longer a man anymore. He was part of the crowd. His eyes were milky white and he was screaming — not screaming in pain, screaming like an animal, a wet gurgling snarl sound that didn't sound human. He was pressed against the glass right where my face had been three minutes before, slamming both palms against it.

I backed away from the door. I backed into the register counter and a rack of lighters fell on the floor. I did not take my eyes off the windows. There were bodies pressed against every pane. Hands slapping. Faces pushed flat against the glass, noses distorted, mouths open showing teeth that were no longer white. The glass was holding. The glass is quarter-inch tempered with a shatterproof laminate, according to the sticker the security company put on it after the last break-in. I had hated that sticker. I could have kissed it.

Then the power went out. The fluorescent lights cut. The soda coolers went quiet. The hum of the slushie machine died mid-hum. The store went dark except for two things: the red neon OPEN sign above the door, flickering on emergency battery, and — outside — a car that had crashed into the gas pump, its headlights still on, shining through the front windows in two bright white beams, casting long shadows across the aisles. The beams of light caught the silhouettes of bodies against the glass. I could see them casting shadows on the floor tiles inside. Shadows with outstretched arms. Shadows with open mouths.

I crawled behind the register counter. I don't remember crawling. One second I was standing there holding a lighter rack, the next I was on my knees behind the counter with my back pressed against it and my phone in my hand. I dialed 911. Fast busy signal. I dialed again. Fast busy. I dialed my mother. Fast busy. I dialed my ex because sometimes panic makes you call people you don't want to call. Fast busy. Every line. Every number. Dead.

I saw the emergency radio under the counter. The one the manager kept for storms. I turned it on. Static. I twisted the dial. One clear signal, cutting in and out. It was the Emergency Broadcast System. That flat monotone robot voice we've all heard a thousand times in test drills. "…a nationwide public health emergency. Remain indoors. Do not attempt to assist others. Do not open your doors to anyone. The infected are extremely aggressive and move at running speed. Bites are lethal. This is not a drill." Then it cut out for ten seconds, and then it came back: "…if you are bitten, you have approximately ninety seconds before full onset. God help us all." That was not in the script. That was the guy who worked the board saying it live. Then silence.

There was a sound from the back of the store. A dragging sound. From the walk-in beer cooler.

I had forgotten about the delivery driver. He had gone into the walk-in to restock beer fifteen minutes before the guy hit the door. His hand truck was still parked in the aisle. I had not seen him come out.

I picked up the baseball bat we kept behind the counter — the one with the sticker on it that said WORLD'S OKAYEST MANAGER. I stood up. I could see the walk-in cooler door down the back aisle, lit only by the headlights coming through the front windows. The cooler door was glass. I could see movement inside.

He was standing against the far wall of the cooler. He was facing me. His uniform was torn at the shoulder. There was blood on his neck. His eyes had gone milky. He was slamming his forehead against the inside of the glass door, slowly, rhythmically, thud, thud, thud. He had been locked in there with the beer for the past fifteen minutes. The cooler had latched from the outside. He was trapped. He wasn't coming out. I wasn't going in.

I went into the storage room. I closed the door behind me and wedged a case of paper towels under the handle. I sat on the floor. I was breathing so hard I could hear myself over the sounds coming from outside. The wet slapping of palms on every window. The snarling. The glass flexing in its frame.

That was the first hour.

Then the neon OPEN sign died. And then the headlights of the car out front died too, because the battery ran out. And the store went completely black. I couldn't see my own hand in front of my face. I could still hear them outside.

That was Day 1.

Tomorrow, I had to figure out how to survive Day 2.

But tonight, I was the guy behind the counter of a gas station, in the dark, holding a baseball bat, with a zombie beer delivery driver locked in the walk-in, and a parking lot full of things that used to be people waiting for the glass to break. I had no plan. I had no food except hot dogs that had been off the roller for four hours. I had no help coming. The radio told me not to open the door for anyone.

I looked at the clock above the register. I couldn't see it in the dark but I knew what it said: 6:54 PM.

Seven minutes after my shift ended.
