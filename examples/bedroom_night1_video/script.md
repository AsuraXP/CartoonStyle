# Night 1 — Trapped in My Bedroom

It is 2:47 in the morning and you are still at your desk.

You were supposed to be asleep three hours ago. Phone died at one. Laptop open to a tab you stopped reading at 1:40. Only light in the room is the yellow desk lamp from the campus bookstore, the warm one that doesn't hurt your eyes. That lamp is the reason you are still alive.

First thing you hear is not a scream. First thing you hear is your front door opening down the hall. Front door opens, front door closes. You live alone. You have lived alone eight months.

Somebody is walking down your hallway in shoes that are not your shoes. Slow. Not in a hurry. They walk like they know exactly where the kitchen is.

You reach for your phone. Your hand closes on the charging cable where the phone is not. It is still dead on the nightstand. It has been dead since one o'clock.

The footsteps stop outside your bedroom door. They do not knock. They do not jiggle the handle. They just stand there. You can hear breathing on the other side. It is not the breathing of a person looking for your laptop. It is the breathing of a person who forgot how breathing works and is doing it from memory.

They walk away. Into the bathroom. Faucet runs fourteen seconds, stops. Toilet lid up, not down. They walk into the living room and they stop.

This is how it starts. Not with a siren. Not with a neighbor screaming. It starts with a stranger using your faucet at 2:49 in the morning and you locked in your bedroom with a dead phone and a desk lamp.

You lock the door. The door doesn't actually lock — it has a push-button that pops if you turn the handle hard enough. You push the button. Then you roll the desk chair under the handle and you immediately learn that a swivel chair on wheels is the worst thing in the room to block a door with. You swap it for the bookshelf. Ikea pine, full of books, takes you a minute to shove across the carpet one silent inch at a time. You push the dresser up against the bookshelf. You lean your mattress against the stack like the world's softest shield.

You turn the lamp off.

In the dark you notice something you never noticed: your door does not meet the carpet. There is a three-quarter inch gap under it. Through the gap you can see light from the living room. The living room light is off. The light coming through the gap is wrong. Gray, thin, the color of nothing.

At 3:14 the footsteps come back. They stop at your door. Through the gap you can see shoes. You know those shoes. They are the sneakers Mr. Henderson wore every morning walking his golden retriever. You have seen them on the landing four times a week for eight months.

Mr. Henderson pushes on the door. The bookshelf doesn't move. He pushes harder. The mattress absorbs it. He walks away.

Here is what you learn over the next seven hours with your ear to the crack. They do not hunt. They return. They come back to the places they were alive, on the days they were there, and they repeat the small things. Mr. Henderson comes to your door at 3:14, 4:32, 5:48, 7:03. A seventy-five minute cycle, like he is knocking on apartments on a route. Between visits he stands in your living room and breathes.

Second problem: the window. Second floor. The lock broke in April and you never fixed it. You lock it now. Wedge a desk ruler in the track. Close the blinds. Then you open the blinds an inch, because if something is going to climb you want to see it first.

Third problem: supplies. You inventory by phone light — the phone is dead but the screen lights up five seconds when you press the button. Half a water on the nightstand. Two granola bars in your backpack. A twelve-pack of soda under the desk from last weekend. Ibuprofen. A textbook you never opened. And your cat, who has been under the bed the entire time. You forgot your cat. The cat is staring at the door. Cats know.

You move everything into the closet. The closet is the deepest place in the room. If the door comes down you want more between you and the hallway than books.

At 8:18 there is tapping on the window. Three quiet taps, pause, two. The dead do not tap. The dead stand and push.

You put your eye to the gap in the blinds. It is the girl from 3B, the one who feeds your cat when you are out of town. Standing on the fire escape, alive, backpack slung over one shoulder, hammer in her belt. One finger over her mouth. You slide the window up three inches.

She whispers that stairs are clear but the lobby is full. She is going roof to roof. She passes you a bottle of water and four AA batteries through the window, says the living are the ones who stayed quiet, and says do not open your door for anything that knocks only once. The living knock three times. She climbs the ladder to the roof and is gone.

The batteries fit a flashlight at the bottom of your backpack. You point it at the ceiling so the light bounces and doesn't reach the gap under the door.

At 9:47 there is a loud crash down the hall. A man's voice — alive, angry — kicking in your neighbor's door. You hear drawers pulled out, the fridge open. For eleven minutes Mr. Henderson's footsteps stay gone because living people make noise and noise moves the dead. Then you hear the man scream once and you hear a crowd of things surging down the hall and that is the end of him. You understand it then. The dead turn toward loud things the way moths turn toward light. You are alive because you have not made a sound in seven hours.

Fourth problem: noise, used on purpose. At 11:00 your clock radio, the one you set once in 2022 and never touched, kicks on by itself. Pop music, full volume, seven seconds before you rip the cord out. Seven seconds is enough. The building comes alive on the other side of the door. Something hits the bookshelf. The mattress holds. You sit in the closet with the cat in your lap and your hand over its mouth for thirty minutes while the crowd drifts away toward louder sounds in other apartments.

You build a weapon out of the loudest thing you own: your twin-bell alarm clock. You tape it to your heaviest textbook. Tie extension cord to the book. Lower it out the window, three inches of glass, hang it just below the sill. Set the alarm for 3:00 PM.

At three o'clock it rings. The bells are the loudest thing the street has heard in twelve hours. You hear a shuffle, a surge, a tide of feet moving away from your door, down the hall, through the lobby, out into the street, chasing the sound. For the first time since 2:47 the other side of your door is silent.

Mr. Henderson comes back at 3:14. His shoes under the gap. He stands a while and walks away because the living room is empty.

Night falls again. Twenty-five hours in the bedroom.

Inventory: person one, cat one, door holding, soda ten, water one and a half bottles, granola bars one, alarm clocks deployed one, textbook lost one, lesson: three knocks is friend, loudness is a weapon.

You write on the wall next to the door in ballpoint, because you will forget:

  NIGHT 1.
  HENDERSON 75 MIN.
  THREE KNOCKS = FRIEND.
  LOUD = SOMEWHERE ELSE.
  CAT: STILL HERE.
  SHIFT NOT OVER.

At 11:17 there is tapping on the window. Three taps, pause, two. The girl from 3B is back with more water and a bag of cat food. She is going to the roof, she says. People up there, radio. Do you want to come.

You look at your door. The bookshelf, the dresser, the mattress, the note on the wall. The cat staring from under the bed. You look at the girl with the hammer in her belt standing on the fire escape under the same moon as last night.

You pick up the cat. You put on the backpack. You turn the desk lamp ON so anything looking at the door still thinks you are sitting in the chair.

You climb out the window.

The door stays closed. The lamp stays on. Night two starts on the roof.
