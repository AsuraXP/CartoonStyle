Is humanity inherently good or evil?
If you've ever watched the news, the answer seems obvious. People lie, steal, fight, start wars. Some days it feels like we are born selfish — and civilization is just a thin coat of paint keeping us from tearing each other apart.
But a baby has never stolen your wallet. A toddler will try to comfort you when you cry, without being taught. Which one is the real human?
Two thousand years ago, philosophers already picked sides. Xunzi in China said people are born bad — greedy, jealous, short-sighted. Without rules and education, we become cruel.
Rousseau, thousands of years later in France, said the opposite. People are born gentle and kind. It is society that poisons us with greed and status.
Who was right?
In the 1960s a psychologist named Stanley Milgram ran a terrifying experiment. He told ordinary people to press a button that delivered a painful electric shock to a stranger in the next room.
Most of them did it. Not because they were monsters. Because a man in a white lab coat told them to. Evil, the experiment seemed to prove, is inside all of us waiting for permission.
Then, twenty years later, another psychologist replicated the study. When one person in the room refused to press the button, ninety percent of the people refused too.
Wait. So we will follow orders into evil, and we will also follow rebellion into goodness?
What are we, actually?
At Yale, scientists showed six-month-old babies a simple puppet show. One puppet helped a climber up a hill. Another pushed the climber down. When given a choice to reach for one puppet, almost every baby grabbed the helper.
Babies, before they can talk, before anyone taught them right and wrong, prefer the person who was kind.
Anthropologists studying small hunter-gatherer tribes have found something else too. For most of the two hundred thousand years humans have existed, we lived in groups where sharing was not optional.
You shared food because if you did not, someone died, and next winter that person would not be there to share with you. We did not survive because we were the strongest animal. We survived because we cooperated.
So we are born kind. Then why are there wars and murders and cruelty?
Because our kindness is small by default. It is for the people right in front of us — our family, our tribe, our village. The problem is not that we are evil. The problem is that our circle of care is too small.
Strangers, outsiders, people who look different, people on the other side of a border — for most of our history those people were dangerous. Our brains learned to fear them.
And that fear, not cruelty, is where most evil starts.
The good news? Our circle grows. Once it was just the family. Then the village. Then the nation. Today, for the first time in history, billions of people care about the suffering of a stranger they will never meet on the other side of the world.
We are not inherently good. We are not inherently evil. We are inherently tribal — built to care for the people close to us, and wary of everyone else.
And the story of human progress is the long slow work of making "close to us" mean more people.
So next time you see the news and think we are terrible, remember: the baby reaching for the helper is also us. The person who refuses to press the button is also us.
We are what we choose to include.
Thanks for watching.
