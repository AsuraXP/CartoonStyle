# The Little Boney Lie: How One Cartoonist Invented the Napoleon Complex

~5 minute explainer, flat-color signature stick figure style.

---

You have a picture of Napoleon in your head right now. Short. Angry. One hand stuffed in his waistcoat. Oversized hat. Yelling. Forever compensating. There's a psychological term named after him: the Napoleon Complex. Short men overachieve because they're insecure about their height. Everyone knows this.

None of it is true. Napoleon wasn't short. The entire story was invented by one guy with a pen, two hundred and twenty years ago — and you still believe it. This is the greatest propaganda coup in history.

Napoleon Bonaparte was born on Corsica in 1769. He went to military school in France. He rose through the ranks during the chaos of the French Revolution. By his mid-twenties he was winning battles. By thirty he was effectively running France. By forty he had crowned himself emperor and conquered most of Europe. He was one of the most successful military commanders who ever lived.

If you ask anyone how tall he was, they'll tell you five foot two. Tiny. Almost a joke. That number comes from his autopsy. His personal doctor wrote it down: five pieds, two pouces. Five foot two.

But here's the thing. Those are French feet and French inches. Before the metric system, every country had its own inches, and a French inch was bigger than an English one. Five-two in French units converts to about five foot six in modern measurements.

Five foot six. That sounds short today. But in 1821, the average French man was about five foot five. Napoleon was slightly above average. Not a giant. But not short. And he definitely wasn't a joke. So how did we get the image of a tiny yelling man?

James Gillray was a British cartoonist working in London in the early 1800s. He is basically the father of the modern political cartoon. Back then political cartoons were not small jokes in a newspaper — they were mass media. Gillray's prints were reproduced by the thousands, posted in taverns and shop windows, passed around like viral memes. Everyone saw them.

The British government hated Napoleon. He was trying to invade them. So in 1803, Gillray published a cartoon called "Maniac Ravings, or Little Boney in a Strong Fit." It showed Napoleon as a tiny, red-faced toddler throwing a tantrum, miniature sword in one hand, flipping over furniture too big for him.

The cartoon was a sensation. It was reprinted everywhere. The nickname "Little Boney" stuck. Gillray made more cartoons, and so did every other British cartoonist after him. They showed Napoleon wearing giant boots, tiny under an enormous hat, dwarfed by his own generals, fighting with a sword that dragged on the ground. He was always small. Always angry. Always a joke.

Napoleon hated these cartoons. He tried to pressure British publishers to stop printing them. He sent diplomats to complain. It didn't work. The British loved it. Years later, in exile, Napoleon said that Gillray "did more than all the armies of Europe to bring me down." That might be an exaggeration. But it tells you how much they stung.

There's one more piece of fuel for the myth: Napoleon's elite Imperial Guard. They were called the Old Guard, and they were the tallest soldiers in the French army. They had to be. Napoleon was always surrounded by very large men in very tall hats. In paintings and newsreels of the time, he looked short by comparison. The cartoonists didn't need to lie — the composition already told their joke for them.

The nickname "Le Petit Caporal" — the Little Corporal — didn't help either. His soldiers called him that, but it was affectionate, like calling a big guy "Tiny." It referred to his rank, not his height. British newspapers translated "petit" literally and added it to the evidence pile.

And then the psychologists showed up. In the 1920s, Alfred Adler coined the idea of the inferiority complex. Other psychologists popularized the term "Napoleon Complex" to describe short men who overcompensate with aggression and ambition. The name itself locked the myth into science. A thing with a medical-sounding name must be real, right?

So let's recap. A unit conversion error. A single cartoonist working for an enemy government. Tall bodyguards making him look shorter by comparison. A nickname lost in translation. A psychologist looking for a famous example. None of these things would stick on their own. But stacked on top of each other over two hundred years, they created one of the most persistent lies in history.

You probably pictured Napoleon as short five minutes ago. You might even still picture him that way tomorrow. That's how good propaganda works. It doesn't make you believe something new. It makes it impossible to stop believing it, even after you know it's not true.

And the cartoonist? James Gillray went blind, went mad, and died in 1815 — the same year Napoleon was finally defeated at Waterloo. He never lived to see his joke outlive empires, cross every ocean, end up in psychology textbooks, and sit in your head two centuries later.

James Gillray never met Napoleon. He never saw him in person. But he drew him small, and for the rest of history, small is what he is.
