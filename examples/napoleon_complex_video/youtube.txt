TITLE
How One Cartoonist Invented the Napoleon Complex (And You Still Believe It)

DESCRIPTION
Close your eyes and picture Napoleon. Short. Angry. One hand in his waistcoat. Oversized hat. Forever compensating. The "Napoleon Complex" is one of the most famous ideas in psychology.

It's also a complete lie. Napoleon Bonaparte wasn't short. The entire myth was invented by a single British cartoonist named James Gillray, armed with a pen, a government that funded wartime propaganda, and a nickname — "Little Boney" — that outlasted empires.

A unit conversion error (French inches were bigger than English inches), a mistranslated military nickname ("Le Petit Caporal" wasn't about his height), a brigade of very tall bodyguards, and one cartoon published in 1803 convinced the entire world that an average-sized man was a tiny raging tyrant. By the 1920s the myth had made it into psychology textbooks as the "Napoleon Complex," and the lie was cemented forever.

Napoleon himself, in exile, supposedly said Gillray "did more than all the armies of Europe to bring me down." Two hundred and twenty years later, you still see him as short. That's how good propaganda works.
