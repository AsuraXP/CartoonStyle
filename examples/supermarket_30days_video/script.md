# Trapped in a Supermarket for 30 Days

~11 minute narrative, flat-color stick figure style.

---

It was 11:47 PM on a Tuesday. I was at my local MegaSave buying toothpaste and frozen pizza, the way you do. The store closes at midnight. I remember checking the self-checkout clock because my pizza had a cook time of 19 minutes and I was doing math about bedtime.

The lights cut to that dim emergency glow. The sliding doors went still. A red SECURED light flicked on above the entrance. I didn't think anything of it at first — the auto-lock was a few minutes fast. I walked up and pushed. The doors didn't move. I looked through the glass. The manager was already in his car driving away. The cashiers were gone. The other customers were gone. I was alone in a supermarket at midnight, locked inside.

Day one was me being annoyed. I banged on the glass. I called the store. Straight to voicemail. I called 911, got through to a dispatcher, said where I was — and then the call dropped because I had one bar of signal and my phone died ten seconds later. I built a nest out of a memory-foam pillow and a comforter from home goods, ate my pizza cold, and slept on a display bed. I thought for sure someone would do a sweep in the morning.

Day two is when I got scared. Morning came through the skylights. No staff. No sweep. No alarm. I walked the whole building. Every emergency exit chained from the outside. Loading dock was a solid metal roll-up. Front doors, same as last night. I threw a jar of pickles at a wall. Then I ate a tub of chocolate ice cream and stared at the ceiling for a long time.

Day three I started thinking like someone who might be here a while. I did inventory. Water — an entire aisle of bottled water, plus drink coolers. Food — produce, dairy, frozen, canned, a whole bakery. I had clothes. I had toilet paper. I had magazines. I had basically everything a person needs to survive, which sounds great until you realize you cannot leave. I moved into the staff break room. It had a couch, a microwave, coffee maker, and a door that locked from the inside. I was now a resident.

Day four I found the janitor's closet. On a shelf, a key ring. I tried them all. Staff washroom — opened. Manager's office — opened. Front door — nothing. Every emergency exit — nothing. Whoever had those keys had taken them home. But the manager's office gave me something: a working security monitor showing every camera in the store. I could see myself standing there, looking at myself on sixteen screens. That was a weird feeling. The landline in there was dead.

Day five I organized the food. Canned goods are forever. Dry pasta and rice basically forever. Frozen only lasts until the power dies. Produce lasts days. Dairy is a ticking clock. Deli meat is a bacterial bomb. I ate all the strawberries first. I built a shelf system from stock carts and labeled things by how long they'd last. It was the most put-together thing I've ever done in my life. I should get locked in a supermarket more often.

Day six I got a pet. The seafood section had a lobster tank. Most of the lobsters looked half-asleep, but one of them was pressed up against the glass like he was watching me. I named him Gerald. I fed him a shrimp from the counter. He took it with his claw. That was the whole origin story. I talked to him constantly. Gerald never talked back, which made him an excellent conversationalist.

Day seven was my first full week. I celebrated. I found sparkling apple cider, microwaved a frozen burrito, set a place for me and a place for Gerald, and gave a speech to an empty store. I said I was going to get out. I said I was going to write a book. I said this was going to make me famous. I drank the whole bottle of cider and passed out at 7:30 PM.

Day eight I tried to escape. I went to the hardware aisle, built a battering ram out of a shopping cart and three cinder blocks, and ran it at the front doors as fast as I could. I hit the glass, bounced off, landed on my back, the cart tipped over. The glass did not crack. I bruised a rib. I did not try that again.

Day nine was boring. That's the part nobody tells you about being trapped — it's mostly boring. I read every magazine, did every crossword puzzle, organized the spice rack alphabetically, and taught myself origami out of receipt paper. I made 47 origami cranes.

Day ten the power went out. Overheads went dark. Refrigeration went quiet. I ran to the seafood section. Gerald's tank aerator ran on electricity and the bubbles had stopped. I grabbed every D battery in the store and MacGyvered the aerator onto a bundle of them using electrical tape and hope. It bubbled. Gerald was saved. I was weirdly proud.

Days eleven through fifteen blur together. The frozen meat thawed and smelled like the worst thing I have ever smelled. I sealed off that aisle with plastic sheeting from paint and duct tape. I lived on canned beans, rice, and crackers. I did thirty perimeter laps of the store every morning — one lap was a third of a mile — and that's how I didn't fully lose my mind. I stopped wearing regular clothes and rotated three pairs of sweatpants.

Day sixteen I logged into the manager's computer. The default password was manager123. It worked. I pulled up the schedule. The store had been permanently closed. Not for the night. Not for renovation. Corporate shut the location down. The last day of business was that Tuesday — my Tuesday. A liquidation crew was supposed to board the place up the next morning. Nobody came. The schedule said CLOSED INDEFINITELY stretching weeks into the future. I sat in that chair for I don't know how long. They didn't forget me. They didn't accidentally lock me in on a regular Tuesday. They closed the whole building, and I was inventory.

Day eighteen I made signs. Big ones. TRAPPED INSIDE. CALL 911. Poster board, permanent marker. I taped them to every window facing the parking lot. I built a flag out of a broomstick and a red shopping bag on the roof door. If anyone looked at the building, they'd see it.

Days nineteen through twenty-two I built things. I made a rain-catchment system under a leaky skylight using shopping bags and a bucket. I moved Gerald into a bigger plastic storage bin with cleaner water. I tried and failed to build a solar phone charger out of phone chargers and hope. I did make a candle reading lamp out of a tin can and cooking oil. It flickered and made the aisles look like a horror movie. I talked to Gerald more than I talked to myself. I gave him a promotion. He was now Store Co-Manager.

Day twenty-four the liquidation crew showed up. A truck pulled into the parking lot. A guy in a uniform got out. I banged on the glass, screamed, waved. He looked at me, stared for a second, then walked to his truck and pulled out plywood and a nail gun. He was boarding the place up. From the outside. Panel by panel. Covering my signs. I screamed until my throat was raw. He either couldn't hear me through the wood or didn't care. He finished, got in his truck, and drove away. The store went almost completely dark.

Day twenty-five I found the camping lanterns, lit them up, made real pasta with jarred sauce on a camp stove, and ate dinner with Gerald by the light. It was the best meal of the thirty days.

Day twenty-six I went for the roof. The roof access door was a regular interior door, not a roll-up. I pried the frame open with a crowbar, climbed a metal ladder, pushed the hatch open, and saw sky for the first time in twenty-six days. No ladder down. But I had poster board and I had markers and I had camp stove fuel. I painted TRAPPED ON ROOF — MEGASAVE — CALL 911 in letters three feet tall. I dragged up everything flammable I could find and lit a fire in a metal trash can. It smoked hard, a black plume visible for miles. I sat on the roof next to that fire for twelve hours.

Day twenty-seven a police helicopter flew over. I waved a sweatpants over my head like a flag. They saw me. Forty-five minutes later there were fire trucks, cop cars, an ambulance, news vans in the parking lot. A firefighter climbed up and helped me down. The first thing I said to the paramedic was "I'm not alone." Two firefighters went back inside and carried Gerald out in his bin.

They checked me out at the hospital. Malnourished, dehydrated, a fractured rib from the shopping cart, but basically fine. I was local news. The CEO of MegaSave apologized and offered me fifty thousand dollars and free groceries for life. I took the fifty thousand. I don't shop at MegaSave anymore.

Gerald went to the city aquarium. I visit him once a month. Lobsters probably don't remember people who rescue them from grocery store tanks. But I visit him anyway. He looks good.

That's the story. I got rescued. Happy ending. Except there's one more thing I didn't realize until much later.

When I was on the roof on day twenty-seven, I looked down at the parking lot, and I could see the front windows from above. The signs I'd taped up on day eighteen were still there. Every one of them. But I had taped them to the inside of the glass — facing inward. The words were backwards from the outside. Mirror letters. TRAPPED was DEPPART. HELP was PLEH. I was yelling for help against a surface that flipped every word.

I don't know how long they were up before the plywood went over them. I don't know if anyone ever stood on the other side of that glass and saw a person screaming and thought it was just a weird poster, a sale sign, decoration, something that didn't say anything at all.

That's the part that haunts me. Not the dark. Not the rotting meat. Not the pickles I threw. That I made signs. I made the signs. I put them up. I thought they said help. But they were backwards the whole time. They say if you're trapped, make yourself visible, signal for help. Nobody tells you to check which side of the glass you're on. I think about that a lot. I think about Gerald swimming in circles in his tank, and I wonder if he ever knew the glass was there. I wonder if we're ever really talking to anyone on the other side, or if we're just yelling backward into a building no one else is in, and we don't find out until it's too late.

Day thirty-one I walked out of the hospital into sunlight. A reporter put a microphone in my face and asked what the first thing I was going to do was. I said I was going to buy toothpaste. It was true — I'd originally gone into MegaSave for toothpaste. I went to a different store. I bought the toothpaste. I went home. I locked the door behind me. I have not been able to sleep with the lights off since.
