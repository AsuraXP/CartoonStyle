# Trapped in a Supermarket for 30 Days (Zombie Edition)

~12:30 absurd dark comedy, flat-color stick figure style, deadpan narration.

---

It was 11:47 PM on a Tuesday. I was at my local MegaSave buying toothpaste. I'd spent nine minutes reading toothpaste labels because there are now forty-seven kinds and I am the person who reads labels. I had selected MAXIMUM CLEAN WITH BAKING SODA AND ENAMEL SHIELD, which sounds like a weapon system. I was heading toward self-checkout.

Then an alarm went off. Not the fire alarm. A deeper, angrier alarm. The lights cut to red emergency glow. The sliding doors slammed shut. Four-inch-thick steel shutters started descending over every window with a sound like a guillotine doing reps. A steel roll-up gate crashed down over the entrance. The PA said SECURITY LOCKDOWN INITIATED. BIOHAZARD PROTOCOL ENGAGED. PLEASE REMAIN CALM. It was a recording that had been waiting in a server somewhere since 1987 for its big day.

I stood there holding my toothpaste. I pushed on the glass. Behind the glass was a steel curtain. I walked the aisles. Nobody. The manager's car was gone. The cashiers' purses were gone. Every single person who worked there had vanished in the forty-five seconds between me grabbing toothpaste and the doors locking. That's how fast people will abandon you during an emergency. They won't even say hey, guy in aisle seven holding toothpaste, you should probably run.

I called 911. The dispatcher said stay where you are sir, do NOT open any doors, do NOT let anyone inside. I said why. She said the situation is evolving. I heard screaming on her end, and then the line went dead. My phone died thirty seconds later because I'd spent all day doomscrolling. Of course I had.

I looked through a two-inch gap in the shutter. In the parking lot, under the streetlights, a person was eating another person. Not sharing a sandwich eating. The kind of eating people don't do. He looked up. His eyes were wrong. He started walking toward the building. That was when I understood this was not shoplifting protocol. This was the zombie apocalypse, and I was in a supermarket.

Day one I made two discoveries. Discovery one: the glass was hurricane-rated impact glass, the Florida kind rated to stop a two-by-four fired from a cannon. I learned this by throwing a jar of pickles at it as hard as I could. The pickles exploded. The glass did not. Discovery two: I should not break the glass even if I could. Because on the other side of the glass were zombies. That is a realization you don't expect to have in your late thirties.

I did inventory. Water — an entire aisle of bottled water. Food — produce, dairy, frozen, canned, a bakery, two aisles of chips. Medicine, clothes, batteries, a hardware aisle, a camping section. Tents. Sleeping bags. A portable grill. I had everything a person needs to survive a zombie apocalypse. I was the luckiest person in the world and also the most trapped. I moved into the staff break room. It locked from the inside. I was now a resident.

Day two I met Gerald. The seafood section had a lobster tank. The aerator had died with the lockdown and most lobsters were floating upside down. One was not. He was pressed against the glass like he was yelling at me, claws up, furious to still be alive. I rigged the aerator onto D batteries with electrical tape and dropped him a shrimp. He ate it while maintaining direct eye contact. I named him Gerald. I made him a name tag: GERALD — CO-MANAGER. We would run this place together.

Day three I started getting comfortable, which is a weird sentence to write about a zombie supermarket lockdown. I built a nest. I made coffee. I found a MegaSave hoodie that said WHERE VALUE LIVES on the back. I did thirty perimeter laps every morning for exercise. Zombies would press their faces against the shutter gaps and watch me jog past. It was the most anyone had ever watched me exercise.

Day five I got the manager's computer working. Default password: manager123. It worked. I had internet for a few hours before the whole town went dark. The outbreak started at a MegaSave distribution center forty miles away. The National Guard had evacuated a fifty-mile quarantine zone. Our store had been auto-locked by a corporate AI panic system. Nobody was coming to sweep. I was listed on the company system as unaccounted for, presumed not present. Presumed. I microwaved a burrito. I sat next to Gerald's tank. We were on our own.

Days six through ten were boring. That's the part zombie movies skip. After the terror, being the last person in a building is mostly just boring. I read every magazine. I did every crossword. I organized the spice rack alphabetically. I taught myself origami out of receipt paper — forty-seven cranes. I held staff meetings with Gerald. I promoted him to Co-President and upgraded his tank to a plastic storage bin with a real aquarium filter. He had more square footage than most Brooklyn studios.

Day eleven the generator died. Refrigeration went quiet. I got Gerald's aerator on battery backup within sixty seconds. I sealed off the frozen foods aisle with plastic sheeting and duct tape because thawing beef smells like hell and the last thing I needed was more zombies drawn by the smell of rotting hamburger.

Days twelve through eighteen I got crafty. Rain catchment under a leaky skylight using shopping bags and a bucket. A lamp made from a tin can and cooking oil that made the aisles look like a horror movie. A failed solar phone charger made from hope and twelve disposable chargers. I pried open the roof access door with a crowbar and found a ladder up to a hatch. I didn't go up yet — I didn't want to meet a zombie on a roof. Life was, if not good, at least stable.

Day nineteen I made a terrible mistake. I decided to signal for help. Poster board, permanent markers. Giant signs: TRAPPED INSIDE. SURVIVOR. CALL 911. Five signs, three feet tall, in letters a helicopter could read. I taped them to the inside of every window facing the parking lot. From inside they looked perfect. I did not think about what they looked like from the outside. They were taped to the inside. The letters faced me. From outside, every word was backwards. Mirror writing. TRAPPED was DEPPART. HELP was PLEH. I did not notice. I had done the thing every trapped person is told to do. I had never been less visible in my life.

Zombies gathered in front of the windows and stared at the signs. They tilted their heads. PLEH. They did not know what PLEH meant. Sometimes they groaned. Sometimes they wandered away. I watched them from behind a cereal display and genuinely thought they could sense me. I felt seen. I was not seen. They were confused by bad typography.

Day twenty-one the National Guard showed up. Convoy trucks, soldiers in hazmat suits, sheets of plywood and nail guns. They were boarding the place up as part of the quarantine. I screamed. I banged on the glass. I waved. They couldn't see me through the shutter gap. They nailed plywood over every window, one panel at a time, covering my signs. One soldier paused, looked in my general direction at a PLEH sign, then went back to nailing. He thought it was gang graffiti. They drove away. The store went almost completely dark. Only the skylights.

Day twenty-five I climbed the roof ladder, pushed the hatch open, saw sky for the first time in twenty-five days. Also a two-story drop and no ladder down. Also no zombies on the roof, which was a plus. I dragged poster board up, markers, a metal trash can, and everything flammable I could find — cardboard, wood, cooking oil, a stack of unwanted romance novels. I painted TRAPPED ON ROOF — SURVIVOR — MEGASAVE in three-foot letters, lit the trash can on fire, and sat next to it for twelve hours watching black smoke billow into the sky.

Day twenty-seven a military helicopter flew over. I waved a sweatpants over my head like a maniac. The spotlight hit me. Forty-five minutes later soldiers were prying plywood off the doors. The first thing I said to a soldier in full hazmat was I'm not alone. He stared. I said there's a lobster. His name is Gerald. He's the co-president. He said sir we've rescued seven hundred people this week and you are the first asking us to save a lobster. Two soldiers carried Gerald out in his bin. He looked annoyed.

Field hospital said I was malnourished, dehydrated, one bruised rib from the pickle jar incident, but basically fine. The MegaSave CEO apologized on TV and offered me fifty thousand dollars and free groceries for life. I took the fifty thousand. I do not shop at MegaSave anymore. I do not shop anywhere after 9 PM. I do not shop anywhere with security shutters.

Gerald went to the city aquarium. I visit him once a month. Lobsters probably don't remember the guy who saved them from a zombie apocalypse lobster tank. But I visit him anyway. He looks very presidential.

That's the story. I got rescued. Happy ending. Except one thing, which is the part that actually haunts me. Not the zombies. Not the rot. Not the canned beans by candlelight. The signs. Six months later I was making toast and I had to sit down. I had taped them to the inside of the glass facing me. Every word was backwards. PLEH. The soldiers saw PLEH and kept nailing. The zombies saw PLEH and tilted their heads. The entire time I thought I was yelling for help, I was yelling gibberish at a world already having the worst week of its life.

I could have died because I taped a piece of paper on the wrong side of a window. They tell you to make yourself visible. Signal. Yell. Put up signs. Nobody adds the most important part: check which side of the glass you're on. I think about Gerald pressing his claw against aquarium glass. I wonder if we're ever really talking to anyone on the other side, or if we're all just yelling backward into a building no one else is in, and we don't find out until it's too late.

The reporter put a microphone in my face and asked what I'd do first. I said buy toothpaste. It was true — I still didn't have any. I went to a smaller store with no security shutters, no steel gates, no biohazard protocol. I bought the toothpaste. I went home. I locked the deadbolt. I put a chair under the handle. I have not slept with the lights off since.

Also, the toothpaste was on sale.
