# Why We Sleep (And What Happens If You Don't)

~4 minute explainer, flat-color signature stick figure style.

---

Tonight, you'll climb into bed, close your eyes, and turn yourself off for eight hours. For a third of your life, you will lie there completely unconscious, completely vulnerable, completely useless — and if you don't do it, you will die. Sleep is the single most dangerous thing you do every single day, and you can't live without it. For thousands of years we had no idea why we did it. Now we're starting to find out, and it's weirder than you think.

If you stay awake long enough, you die. That's not a metaphor. In the 1980s a scientist named Allan Rechtschaffen kept rats awake on a little spinning platform over water. After two weeks they were all dead. Their hair fell out. Their skin developed lesions. They lost weight even though they were eating more. Their immune systems stopped working. Their bodies just gave out. And Rechtschaffen still couldn't tell you exactly why sleep was the thing that killed them. We still can't — not fully. But we've learned a lot.

In 1963 a seventeen-year-old named Randy Gardner stayed awake for high school science fair. Eleven days. Two hundred and sixty-four hours. On day two his eyes stopped focusing. On day three he was moody and uncoordinated. By day five he was hallucinating. He thought he was a famous football player. He thought a street sign was a person. When he finally fell asleep he slept for fifteen hours straight, then another ten, then he went back to normal. He survived, mostly. Do not try this. Sleep is not optional.

So what is sleep actually doing? For a long time people thought your brain just turned off at night. It doesn't. Your brain is *more* active during some parts of sleep than it is when you're awake. It cycles about every ninety minutes between deep sleep and REM sleep. Deep sleep is the slow, heavy part, and REM — rapid eye movement — is when you dream. You do both every single night, and both are doing different jobs.

Deep sleep is when your brain does its janitorial work. A few years ago scientists discovered something called the glymphatic system — basically a network of tiny pipes that only switches on when you're asleep. While you're awake, your brain cells are busy, and they produce waste the way every busy factory does. During deep sleep those brain cells literally shrink to make space between them, and cerebrospinal fluid washes through in pulses, flushing out all the garbage. The biggest piece of garbage is a protein called beta-amyloid. Build up too much of it, and you get the plaques associated with Alzheimer's. Every bad night of sleep is a skipped trash day for your brain.

Sleep also files your memories. While you're awake everything you see and hear and learn gets dumped into a kind of temporary inbox called the hippocampus. During deep sleep, your brain replays those memories at high speed and moves the important ones into long-term storage in the cortex. The boring stuff gets deleted. That's why pulling an all-nighter before an exam doesn't work — you might cram the information in for an hour, but without sleep your brain never saves it.

Then there's REM sleep — the weird part. That's when you dream. Your brain paralyzes your body so you don't act out whatever is happening. Your eyes dart around under your eyelids like you're watching something. Your heart rate goes up. Emotionally charged parts of your brain light up like a Christmas tree while the logical prefrontal cortex goes dark. We think this is where emotional processing happens — your brain runs through scary or upsetting or exciting things that happened during the day but without the noradrenaline that makes them stressful, basically stripping the emotion off the memory so you can remember it without reliving it. Dreams may be the side effect of that processing: your visual cortex trying to assemble a story out of random memory fragments being sorted and filed.

So how much sleep do you actually need? The answer for almost every adult is somewhere between seven and nine hours. Not six. Five hours of sleep a night for one week leaves you as impaired as someone with a blood alcohol level of zero point one — legally drunk. You just don't notice, because you've gotten used to feeling like garbage. Caffeine doesn't fix it, it just hides the sleep debt from you for a few hours. Sleeping five hours during the week and trying to catch up on the weekend doesn't fully fix it either — the damage accumulates.

The weirdest thing? For most of human history, people didn't sleep eight hours straight. Before electric light, bedtime was when the sun went down. People slept for about four hours, woke up for an hour or two in the middle of the night — they read, they prayed, they talked, they visited neighbors — and then slept another four. Eight hours in one block is actually a relatively modern invention, made normal by artificial light and factory schedules. But that doesn't mean it's bad. It just means your brain is flexible.

The big picture: sleep isn't a luxury. It isn't wasted time. It's when your brain cleans itself, files your memories, processes your emotions, and gets you ready for tomorrow. Skip it and every part of you gets worse — your memory, your reaction time, your mood, your immune system, your judgment, even your ability to tell how tired you are. So tonight, close your laptop. Put the phone away. Go to bed. Your brain has a lot of cleaning to do.
